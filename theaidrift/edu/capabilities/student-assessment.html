Near (2026–2031): For student assessment broadly, the process of assessment delivery is shifting toward iterative human-AI writing cycles where students employ AI as a collaborative partner rather than a simple generation tool [1]. This change in delivery mechanism introduces a risk to educational substance through “cognitive surrender,” where users may abandon the logical thinking and research processes that assessments are intended to measure [5], while a trend toward the homogenization of writing and thought patterns may reduce the ability of assessors to identify individual critical voice during assessment marking and feedback [9]. In assessment administration, the reliability of authorship verification is constrained by technical failures in detection tools, including the production of false positives [13] and the emergence of “humanizer” tools that allow students to bypass detection at a sentence level [15]. The validity of the essay as a primary instrument for measuring competency in mass education systems is being questioned as generative AI exposes structural flaws in artifact-based delivery [3]. To address this, new LLM-based protocols, such as Vantage, are being proposed to move assessment delivery toward the measurement of higher-order capabilities like collaboration, creativity, and critical thinking [17]. For a subset of institutions implementing AI in STEM and vocational training, the process of assessment marking and feedback is incorporating the automation of low-stakes mock exams [8] and the requirement for human-led auditing to verify the logical truth of AI-generated code [7]. Furthermore, a regulatory movement toward the explainability and certification of AI-generated educational assessments is emerging to ensure that scalable item creation does not compromise pedagogical validity [18]. High-stakes, invigilated examinations are unlikely to change in the near term due to the rigid requirements of professional accreditation regimes [19].

Hypothesis: In the near term, assessment delivery may shift toward iterative human-AI collaboration and new protocols for higher-order skills, while assessment administration is increasingly constrained by the technical failure of AI detection tools.

Mid (2036–2041): For student assessment broadly: If current trajectories hold, the educational substance of assessment delivery may shift from the evaluation of a final static artifact to the evaluation of the iterative process of AI interaction and prompting [1][3]. Extrapolating from evidence of cognitive surrender and the failure of detection tools [5][15], the process of marking and feedback may move toward assessing a student’s capacity to audit, verify, and correct synthetic errors as the primary proxy for logical reasoning [5][7]. This would transition the capability from measuring product output to measuring the student’s ability to steer and validate synthetic agents [14]. For a subset of institutions utilizing multimodal cognitive monitoring, assessment results management may shift toward the longitudinal mapping of cognitive effort and engagement via educational fMRIs and similar multimodal AI [4]. The basic administrative recording of credits and the formal transmission of final results will likely persist unchanged as these are low-variance institutional functions with high regulatory inertia.

Hypothesis: In the mid term, the substance of student assessment may shift from measuring product output to measuring the student’s ability to audit and steer synthetic agents.

Far (2046–2051): For student assessment broadly: One plausible outcome is that assessment delivery and results management move entirely toward continuous, multimodal monitoring of cognitive development, rendering discrete, point-in-time assessments obsolete [4][14]. A rival outcome is that the persistent failure of synthetic verification leads to a return to strictly analog, high-stakes oral examinations and handwritten assessments to ensure human cognitive agency. It remains unknown whether AI-driven certification can achieve the social legitimacy currently provided by human-led accreditation. The institutional requirement for a degree as a credential of social signaling will likely resist change even at this horizon.

Hypothesis A: In the far term, student assessment may transition from discrete event-based testing to continuous, multimodal cognitive monitoring.

Hypothesis B (competing): In the far term, a crisis of synthetic validity may force a return to analog, invigilated assessment methods to preserve the legitimacy of the credential.
