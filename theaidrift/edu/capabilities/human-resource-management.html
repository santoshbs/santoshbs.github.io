Mid (2036–2041): For human resource management broadly, if current trajectories in agentic automation hold, the scope of talent management may expand from initial onboarding to the end-to-end orchestration of employee development pipelines [10]. Extrapolating from current trials, workforce planning may see a shift in the substance of remuneration and entitlements management as some institutions decouple pay from fixed hourly requirements in response to AI-driven productivity gains [5]. In the subsegment of institutions utilizing AI recruitment, the process may shift toward mandatory algorithmic auditing to mitigate the risks of systemic bias and assisted evaluation errors [1][9]. Tenure-track hierarchies are likely to persist because they are deeply embedded in academic governance, faculty contracts, and the social legitimacy of the professoriate. The requirement for human oversight in final hiring decisions remains unchanged because the social legitimacy of academic appointments relies on peer-led evaluation and disciplinary consensus.

Hypothesis: In the mid term, AI in HR may transition from experimental toolsets to regulated utilities, where the primary constraints are the mitigation of historical bias and the verification of biometric authenticity.

Far (2046–2051): For human resource management broadly, the long-term state depends on whether regulatory frameworks can keep pace with generative complexity. One plausible outcome involves the institutionalization of audited recruitment and onboarding pipelines where bias is mathematically minimized and staff identity is cryptographically verified [1][2][9]. A rival outcome is a return to high-touch, human-centric curation in “elite” institutional tiers as a way to signal social prestige and avoid the perceived homogeneity of algorithmic talent management. It remains unknown whether AI can effectively model the qualitative nuances of “cultural fit” without reinforcing the very biases it was designed to eliminate [1][9]. The fundamental requirement for human-led dispute resolution in workforce relations management is expected to resist change even at this horizon, as the legitimacy of labor arbitration depends on human empathy and perceived fairness.

Hypothesis B (competing): In the far term, the capability fragments, with high-status institutions reverting to human-exclusive HR processes to maintain social legitimacy and distinctiveness.
