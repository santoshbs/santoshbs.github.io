Near (2026–2031): For a subset of researchers in computational, biomedical, and mathematical sciences, and in specific regions including Japan and the UK, AI is altering the delivery mechanisms and educational substance of research production, dataset management, and output management. In research production for the life sciences, delivery is shifting toward autonomous execution, where AI designs and runs lab experiments [19], accelerates drug discovery through petabyte-scale data scanning [8], employs domain-specific bioinformatics toolsets [21], and automates the design of deep learning architectures for biomedical image segmentation [26], supported by new benchmarks for autonomous hypothesis generation [28]. In computational research production, delivery is accelerated by the automation of GPU kernel optimization [4], the capacity for full-precision training of 100B+ parameter models on single GPUs [13], and reduced energy requirements [6], while routine software engineering tasks are increasingly automated via agentic coding tools [5][22] and cost-effective infrastructure [17]. Theoretical research production in mathematics is shifting in substance toward a reliance on compute-intensive engineering over mathematically principled architecture design [2][30], while adopting AI-mediated common languages for communication and proof [15]. Research output management delivery is shifting toward automated manuscript synthesis via multi-agent frameworks [18] and custom extraction pipelines for literature reviews [7], though general-purpose AI research tools [23] introduce substance risks regarding the authenticity and ownership of synthesized text [12]. Research dataset management is diverging both regionally—where Japan’s relaxed privacy laws accelerate data availability [10] while the UK faces systemic hurdles in centralizing public data [11]—and technically, as delivery shifts from centralized aggregation toward privacy-preserving federated learning [24], “outcome routing” for small-N rare disease datasets [25], and optimized annotation workflows [9]. However, behavioral science research production faces a substance risk where LLM behavioral simulators lack causal validity [3], a failure mode linked to “cognitive surrender” where users abandon logical verification [1]. The institutional peer-review process for research output management is unlikely to change yet, as it remains the primary mechanism for professional accountability and social legitimacy.

Hypothesis: In the near term, research delivery within AI-reliant subsegments will experience a tension between accelerated technical output and a decline in human-led logical and causal verification.

Mid (2036–2041): For these same subsegments and regions, if current trajectories hold, research production may shift toward a verification-centric model. Because “cognitive surrender” [1] and causally invalid simulations [3] erode the validity of automated results, the substance of research production may shift from primary discovery to rigorous verification. The combination of automated drafting [18] and agentic workflows [20] could necessitate the integration of documented human-in-the-loop validation steps, particularly in healthcare and life sciences [16], within research output management to maintain scientific legitimacy. In technical research production, the emergence of AI-to-AI communication protocols [14] may allow autonomous agents to handle the iterative loop of hypothesis testing and refinement, further decoupling research production from manual coding expertise. Despite these shifts, the fundamental requirement for human authors to assume legal and ethical responsibility for research outputs will likely persist due to institutional liability frameworks.

Hypothesis: In the mid term, the capability of research production will bifurcate into autonomous agentic execution for routine experimentation and human-led audit for causal validation.

Far (2046–2051): For the identified subsegments, the scope of research delivery may either fully automate or fundamentally contract. If AI-to-AI protocols [14] and autonomous lab execution [19] achieve high reliability, research production could shift to a model of bounded autonomy where humans only define high-level objectives and audit final proofs. However, a rival outcome exists where a “verification crisis”—driven by the cumulative erosion of causal understanding [3]—leads to a retreat from AI-driven production in favor of “provably human” research to restore trust in scientific substance. A significant unknown remains the extent to which quantum computing may disrupt AI-based compute centers [29], potentially altering the energy and hardware constraints of research production. Even at this horizon, the social requirement for peer-led consensus in the validation of new knowledge is expected to resist full automation.

Hypothesis A: In the far term, research delivery becomes an orchestration capability where humans manage fleets of autonomous research agents across the full production-to-output lifecycle.

Hypothesis B (competing): In the far term, the failure of AI to maintain causal validity leads to a systemic re-valuation of manual, human-led research production as the only legitimate source of scientific truth.
