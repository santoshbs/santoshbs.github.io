Near (2026–2031): For buyers and purchasing agents of farm products broadly, the adoption of digital procurement platforms may reduce the manual labor involved in transactional workflows [1]. Because virtual cards and automated spend controls replace manual purchase order creation and payment reconciliation, the task to maintain records of business transactions and product inventories shifts toward automated data capture [1]. This mechanism allows for real-time spend visibility, potentially altering how agents purchase farm products for resale by streamlining the payment phase of the procurement cycle [1]. However, the task to negotiate contracts with farmers is unlikely to change in this period because the interpersonal persuasion and trust required for agricultural production agreements remain outside the scope of spend-management software [2]. Similarly, the need to examine or test crops to estimate value remains a physical, embodied requirement that resists digitalization [2].

Hypothesis: In the near term, transactional record-keeping and payment processing for farm product procurement will shift from manual workflows to automated digital spend-management systems.

Mid (2036–2041): For this occupation broadly, if current trajectories hold, the integration of spend-management data with demand forecasting may alter the process to review orders to determine product types and quantities [1][2]. As procurement platforms capture more granular transaction data, the mechanism for identifying supply gaps may shift from agent-led estimation to data-driven alerts, narrowing the agent’s role in the initial ordering phase [1]. Despite this, the task to arrange for transportation or storage of purchased products is likely to persist unchanged due to the high site variability and physical constraints of agricultural logistics [2]. The coordination of workers engaged in cutting or milling remains dependent on localized, real-time physical oversight [2].

Hypothesis: In the mid term, the task of reviewing orders for demand will be increasingly driven by automated procurement data, while physical logistics and site coordination remain manually managed.

Far (2046–2051): For this occupation broadly, the long-term trajectory depends on whether procurement automation extends into quality verification. If sensing technologies integrate with spend-management platforms, the mechanism for estimating crop value could shift from manual inspection to automated grading, potentially reducing the need for agents to physically examine products [2]. However, it remains unknown if regulatory regimes regarding government grain quotas will permit fully automated purchasing without human certification [2]. The high-stakes nature of long-term farmer-buyer relationships suggests that contract negotiation will resist full automation even at this horizon [2].

Hypothesis A: In the far term, the occupation evolves into a strategic portfolio management role where transactional and quality-assessment tasks are automated, leaving only high-level contract negotiation.

Hypothesis B (competing): In the far term, the occupation splits into a bifurcated market where corporate agents use fully automated procurement stacks, while specialty agents maintain a traditional, embodied practice centered on physical inspection and relationship-based sourcing.
