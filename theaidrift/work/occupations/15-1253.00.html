Mid (2036–2041): For testers in high-complexity and safety-critical environments: if current trajectories hold, the task of updating automated test scripts to ensure currency may shift toward managing dynamic, model-generated updates informed by AI performance predictions [11]. Participating in product design reviews to provide input on functional requirements will likely persist unchanged because it depends on human negotiation of schedules and stakeholder priorities [2].

Hypothesis: In the mid term, the analyst’s role in high-complexity environments may transition toward the orchestration of automated simulations and the high-volume triage of AI-generated defect reports.

Far (2046–2051): For testers in high-reliability and large-scale engineering settings: one plausible outcome is that LLMs and automated reasoning automate the validation of compliance-heavy test suites, reducing the analyst’s role to auditing LLM-generated evidence [1][12]. A rival outcome is that the non-deterministic nature of AI systems creates an infinite state space that resists full automation, requiring analysts to shift toward adversarial probing and systemic fragility analysis [7]. It remains unknown whether formal verification can scale to the entirety of evolving, interconnected software ecosystems. Final sign-off on product release readiness is likely to resist change due to the legal and liability constraints associated with certifying safety-critical software [2].

Hypothesis A: In the far term, the role may evolve into a high-level compliance auditor who validates the formal proofs generated by automated reasoning systems.

Hypothesis B (competing): In the far term, the role may shift toward an adversarial specialist focused on identifying systemic failure modes that automated tools cannot predict.
