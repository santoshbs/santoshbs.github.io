Near (2026–2031): For a subset of workers in experimental neuro-prosthetic clinics, the integration of brain-computer interfaces (BCI) may alter the task of fitting, testing, and evaluating devices on patients [1][2]. Because BCI implants allow users to perceive robotic limbs as part of their own body [1], the evaluation process for this subsegment must expand to include the calibration of sensory feedback loops rather than focusing solely on mechanical fit [2]. The task of instructing patients in the use and care of prostheses may shift to include neural control training and sensory adaptation protocols [1][2]. However, the fabrication of standard passive orthoses and basic bracing is unlikely to change in this window because BCI research remains concentrated on complex robotic limb replacement rather than supportive orthopedic appliances [2].

Hypothesis: A small subset of practitioners in specialized research settings may integrate neural calibration into their fitting and evaluation workflows in the near term.

Mid (2036–2041): For the same subset of workers in specialized neuro-prosthetic settings, if current trajectories in neural implants hold, the task of designing orthopedic and prosthetic devices may become more dependent on neuro-anatomical data [1][2]. Extrapolating from early BCI research, clinicians may need to align device sensor arrays with specific neural implant configurations to maintain the user’s sense of limb ownership [1]. This requires a shift in how practitioners confer with physicians to formulate specifications, moving toward a multi-disciplinary neural-mechanical prescription [2]. The use of plaster casts for basic socket shaping is likely to persist unchanged because neural control does not eliminate the physical requirement for a stable, non-destructive interface between the residual limb and the device [2].

Hypothesis: For practitioners in advanced prosthetic clinics, device design and physician consultation may increasingly incorporate neural interface specifications in the mid term.

Far (2046–2051): For workers in advanced prosthetic settings, the primary constraint in device fitting may shift from mechanical alignment to the optimization of the neural-digital bridge [1][2]. One outcome is that sensory-integrated robotic limbs become the standard for high-end prosthetics, making neural calibration a core competency for this subsegment. A rival outcome is that BCI implants remain restricted to niche populations with severe paralysis due to long-term biocompatibility failures or surgical risks, leaving most prosthetists to rely on non-invasive myoelectric controls [1]. It remains unknown whether implant stability can be maintained over decades without significant signal degradation. Even at this horizon, the need to measure patients to identify factors affecting appliance fit is unlikely to disappear, as skin integrity and pressure distribution remain critical biological constraints [2].

Hypothesis A: Neural-integrated fitting may become the dominant design constraint for robotic limbs in specialized clinical settings in the far term.

Hypothesis B (competing): BCI-driven prosthetics may remain a marginal specialty, with the broader subsegment continuing to use non-invasive interfaces in the far term.
