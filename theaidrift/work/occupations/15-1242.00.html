Mid (2036–2041): For database administrators broadly, if current trajectories in predictive provisioning hold, the task of selecting and entering codes to monitor database performance could move toward an auditing role [1], [2]. Practitioners may spend less time on routine hardware recommendation and more time validating the accuracy of AI-driven allocation models to prevent under-provisioning during peak loads [1]. The necessity to correct errors and make necessary modifications to databases persists, as these tasks often involve resolving complex logical conflicts that predictive storage tools cannot address [2].

Hypothesis: In the mid term, the role may evolve from active capacity provisioning to the auditing and validation of automated performance-tuning systems.

Far (2046–2051): For database administrators broadly, the long-term trajectory suggests two rival outcomes based on the evolution of database architecture. In one scenario, the abstraction of the storage layer via AI could render the task of recommending hardware technologies obsolete, shifting the occupation toward high-level data governance and the development of standards for software acquisition [2]. In a competing scenario, the increasing complexity of hyper-scale environments may create a niche for specialists who manually override AI to manage extreme performance edge-cases [1]. It remains unknown whether future database management systems will fully decouple logical data modeling from physical resource allocation. The fundamental need to train users and answer questions about data access is likely to resist change, as it remains tied to human organizational hierarchies [2].

Hypothesis A: In the far term, the occupation may transition into a governance-focused role as routine performance and capacity tasks become fully automated.

Hypothesis B (competing): In the far term, the occupation may bifurcate, with a small class of high-specialization engineers managing the physical constraints that AI cannot optimize.
