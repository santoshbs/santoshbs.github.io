This shift requires a modification of how workers compute final estimation of property values by introducing new comparative benchmarks for last-mile distribution assets versus traditional big-box industrial properties [1], [2]. The mechanism is a change in the “highest and best use” analysis, where smaller, service-oriented footprints now command different market premiums than larger warehouses [1]. Because professional standards require physical verification of asset condition, the task of inspecting properties to determine appraisal value is unlikely to change [2].

Hypothesis: In the near term, industrial real estate valuation will shift toward more granular, format-specific comparative market analyses to account for the rise of last-mile distribution assets.

Mid (2036–2041): If current trajectories in industrial fragmentation hold, for appraisers and assessors of real estate broadly, valuation models may transition from simple square-footage metrics to higher-resolution utility analysis. This shift could affect the task of analyzing trends in sales prices and rents, as the value of shallow-bay properties becomes more dependent on hyper-local logistics networks than regional industrial trends [1], [2]. However, the requirement to search public records for transactions remains a structural necessity to ensure the accuracy of the data used in these updated models [2].

Hypothesis: In the mid term, the occupation may see a divergence in valuation methodologies between bulk industrial assets and specialized, high-utility urban logistics nodes.

Far (2046–2051): For appraisers and assessors of real estate broadly, the persistence of the shallow-bay trend may lead to a fundamental decoupling of industrial property value from traditional zoning categories. One rival outcome involves a shift toward dynamic valuation based on real-time throughput data, while another suggests a consolidation back to larger hubs if distribution technology evolves beyond last-mile vehicle dependencies. It remains unknown how evolving urban zoning laws will reconcile industrial shallow-bay demand with residential proximity. The task of testifying in court as to the value of a piece of real estate is likely to resist change because the legal system requires a named professional to assume liability for the valuation [2].
