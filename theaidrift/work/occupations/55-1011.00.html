Air Crew Officers

O*NET Code: 55-1011.00

Near (2026–2031): For a subset of workers in combat and mobility aircrews, and officers within large-scale military aviation organizations, operational changes are driven by platform modernization, shifted training mandates, and evolving theater risks. In combat-oriented squadrons, the use of AI programs such as Project Maven may alter the task of operating aircraft weapons and defensive systems by requiring officers to validate automated target identifications [2]. The procurement of Collaborative Combat Aircraft (CCA) may shift the task of operating aircraft communications and radar equipment to include the tactical coordination of autonomous drones [3]. For officers operating in highly contested airspaces, as reported in Iran, the task of directing in-flight duties involves navigating higher risks of aircraft damage and the failure of air superiority assumptions [7]. For mobility aircrews, the adoption of “moving map” prototypes may update how officers establish situational awareness in aircraft lacking integrated digital displays [4]. In regions with increased legacy platform deployments, such as the Middle East, officers may face a higher frequency of conducting preflight, in-flight, and postflight inspections of onboard equipment to maintain aircraft readiness [1]. In certain military organizations, as seen in the U.S. Army, the task of instructing and mentoring trainee pilots may be shifted from internal officers to outsourced private firms [6], while other officers perform the task of validating technical aircraft standards during flight school competitions [5]. The introduction of new platform classes, such as tiltrotor aircraft, requires officers to manage the tactical deployment and safety protocols of non-traditional airframes [9]. High-value retention bonuses are being utilized in some services to extend the tenure of experienced aviators [8]. The task of directing cargo and personnel drops is unlikely to change yet due to the stability of logistical requirements and a lack of current signals indicating autonomous cargo delivery at scale [2].

Hypothesis: In the near term, combat Air Crew Officers will increasingly manage mixed manned-unmanned teams and AI-assisted targeting, while some officers in large military organizations will shift from pilot instruction toward technical platform validation.

Mid (2036–2041): For combat-oriented aircraft squadrons: if current trajectories in CCA procurement hold, the role of the officer may shift from direct platform operation to the tactical orchestration of multiple autonomous systems [3]. Extrapolating from the deployment of AI targeting tools, the task of operating aircraft weapons and defensive systems could evolve into a supervisory function where the officer manages AI-driven engagement options rather than executing manual targeting [2]. The requirement for human judgment in validating rules of engagement is likely to persist because of the catastrophic error cost associated with combat operations [2]. The necessity of onboard personnel for executing electronic warfare and directing in-flight duties in contested environments may resist automation if satellite linkages remain vulnerable to jamming and air superiority remains unstable [1][7].

Hypothesis: In the mid term, the role of combat Air Crew Officers may transition toward a “battle manager” model, focusing on the coordination of autonomous wingmen and the supervision of AI-driven weapon systems.

Far (2046–2051): For combat-oriented aircraft squadrons: the role of the onboard crew remains uncertain as autonomous capabilities mature. Rival outcomes depend on whether regulatory regimes allow for fully autonomous lethal force or maintain a strict “human-in-the-loop” requirement for operating aircraft weapons and defensive systems [2][3]. It remains unknown if the “loyal wingman” model will eventually eliminate the requirement for a manned lead aircraft or if the persistence of highly contested airspaces will necessitate human presence for real-time adaptation. The need for human strategic command during high-stakes geopolitical engagements is expected to resist change even at this horizon.

Hypothesis A: In the far term, the occupation may split into distinct roles of remote strategic commanders and specialized onboard technicians who maintain hardware in extreme environments.

Hypothesis B (competing): In the far term, strict regulatory mandates for human accountability in lethal force may preserve the current onboard officer structure despite the availability of full autonomy.
